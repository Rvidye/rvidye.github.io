*A little archive of my adventures in real-time computer graphics.*

Before getting into graphics research, I spent quite a few years experimenting with real-time rendering, building demos, implementing visual effects, and occasionally spending days debugging something that turned out to be a single line of code.

Here are some of my favorite projects from those days. Some turned out pretty well, others... not so much. But I had a lot of fun making them.

## 1. Superposition

<YouTube id="t7KAha8s1iE" title="Superposition, an OpenGL demo" poster={superposition} />

Inspired by UNIGINE's Superposition benchmark, this demo was my attempt to test the limits of OpenGL and, more importantly, my patience.

It eventually became my final project presentation at AstromediComp's RTR 5 batch, presented on January 4th, Isaac Newton's birthday. A rather fitting tribute to the mathematician whose work makes half of computer graphics possible.

There's a lot going on here, including some rather questionable physics.

**Effects:** Voxel Global Illumination (VXGI), Physically Based Rendering (PBR), Screen Space Reflections (SSR), Screen Space Ambient Occlusion (SSAO), Exponential Shadow Maps (ESM), Volumetric Lighting, and Temporal Anti-Aliasing (TAA).

## 2. A Mother's Day Special

<YouTube id="uWI0NRxRAA8" title="A Mother's Day Special, a WebGL demo" poster={mothersDay} />

I wanted to make something memorable for Mother's Day, so I ended up creating this little wholesome scene entirely in WebGL.

I'm still surprised I managed to finish it because WebGL almost made me lose my mind. I kept running into DirectX 11 errors through ANGLE, particularly around shader optimizations. Apparently, just because the specification says something should work doesn't mean the underlying graphics driver agrees.

But at least the final result is cute.

**Effects:** Cel Shading, Geometry Outlines, Procedural Grass, Terrain Rendering, and Water.

## 3. DirectX 11 Demo

<YouTube id="xTq7zJkaFBU" title="A collaborative DirectX 11 demo" poster={directx11} />

Directed by **Dr. Vijay Gokhale**, this was a collaborative DirectX 11 project involving 27 people, including me.

I spent most of my time working on the rendering engine's architecture, keeping everything performant, and making sure the project didn't spontaneously combust every time someone committed new code.

Coordinating an engine with 26 other developers, some of whom were still getting comfortable with C++, was an interesting experience, to say the least.

I probably learned as much about software engineering and debugging other people's code as I did about rendering.

## 4. A New Home

<YouTube id="qMjk4k_YVFo" title="A New Home, a rendering demo" poster={aNewHome} />

This demo was probably the beginning of my obsession with **Global Illumination**, especially when working on the first indoor scene.

I built it with two of my close friends, and we had a great time experimenting with lighting, environments, and storytelling.

Does the story make any sense? Not really.

Does it look cool AF? Absolutely.

## 5. A Tribute to Guru Dutt

<YouTube id="6yABiQ7ILKQ" title="A Tribute to Guru Dutt, a WebGL demo" poster={guruDutt} />

Another WebGL experiment, this time attempting to pay tribute to the legendary Indian filmmaker **Guru Dutt**.

I distinctly remember us thinking we were creating an absolute masterpiece.

Looking back at it now, I'm not entirely sure what we were thinking.

It's a pretty depressing watch, so consider yourself warned.

## 6. The Cinephile

<YouTube id="inNrDcDrMUo" title="The Cinephile, a student demo running on a PSP emulator" poster={cinephile} />

*Student project.* This one isn't my project, but I wanted to include it because I'm genuinely proud of the students who made it.

**The Cinephile** was developed by students I mentored, and it runs on **PPSSPP, a PlayStation Portable emulator**.

For context, the original PSP had a 166 MHz graphics processor, a theoretical throughput of around 4 million polygons per second, and just 2 MB of embedded eDRAM for graphics (32 MB in total).

Seeing what they managed to achieve within those hardware constraints is pretty impressive. I had no part in developing the demo itself, but it's easily one of my favorite student projects.
