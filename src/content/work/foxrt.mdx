FoxRT is a spectral volumetric path tracer. The name is a play on FoxEngine, my real-time rendering engine.

In Fall 2025 I started my master's at the University of Utah with [CS 6620: Rendering with Ray Tracing](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/courses/cs6620/fall2025/), which is, in my opinion, one of the best courses on the subject.

I'd experimented with ray tracing before, through Peter Shirley's [*Ray Tracing in One Weekend*](https://raytracing.github.io/) series, and had tried to work through [*Physically Based Rendering: From Theory to Implementation*](https://pbr-book.org/) (PBRT), but its sheer scope always made it hard to follow. The course finally gave me the foundation to work through the book properly.

I'd been interested in volumetric light transport for a long time (my [OpenVDB volume path tracer](/work/openvdb-volume-tracer/) is from 2022), so for my final project I decided to study PBRT's [volumetric path integrator](https://pbr-book.org/4ed/Light_Transport_II_Volume_Rendering/Volume_Scattering_Integrators).

## The first attempt

My first implementation extended the course framework to load VDB volumes and path trace them by brute force: fixed-step ray marching with a cubic reconstruction filter.

For the first round of the [Teapot Rendering Competition](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/trc/?year=2025), I rendered a cloud shaped like the Utah teapot, using a volume from [pbrt-v4-volumes](https://github.com/shadeops/pbrt-v4-volumes).

</Figure>

The result looked pretty cool. It also took **7 hours** to render a single image with one volume and HDRI lighting: 1280 × 720 at 2048 samples per pixel, with a Halton sampler, a Gaussian pixel filter of radius 2.5, and paths up to 50 bounces deep ([project page](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/courses/cs6620/fall2025/?prj=14&u=u1591868)).

Clearly, there was room for improvement.

## Starting over

The original renderer had two big problems.

**Scene creation was painful.** Throughout the course we described scenes in XML files. That was fine for assignments, but increasingly frustrating for complex scenes, and for animating anything at all.

**Performance was terrible.** Some questionable architectural decisions I'd made early on had caught up with me: my multithreaded renderer was somehow slower than the single-threaded one.

So rather than keep patching things together, I wrote FoxRT from scratch. This time I followed my notes from PBRT more closely, designed the renderer around spectral sampling, and structured the computation with SIMD and AVX-512 in mind. I also replaced fixed-step volume marching with [delta tracking](https://pbr-book.org/4ed/Light_Transport_II_Volume_Rendering/The_Equation_of_Transfer), which made volumetric rendering far more efficient.

To fix scene creation, I wrote a [Houdini](https://www.sidefx.com/products/houdini/) integration, so I could build, modify and animate scenes without editing XML by hand.

## The results

For the final competition submission I made **Nested Teapot**: a LEGO teapot inside a glass teapot, with wavelength-dependent dispersion in the glass.

<Figure src={nestedTeapot} alt="A glass Utah teapot with a LEGO room inside. A minifigure sits on a blue sofa beside a small red LEGO teapot, and the glass refracts the toys behind it.">
  Nested Teapot, rendered with FoxRT.

With the new renderer and the Houdini workflow, all 120 frames of the animation rendered in about 5 hours, at 1280 × 720 and 1024 samples per pixel ([project page](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/courses/cs6620/fall2025/?prj=15&u=u1591868)).

## From teapots to scientific visualization

The following semester I took [CS 6635: Visualization for Scientific Data](https://my.eng.utah.edu/~cs6635/), and FoxRT found another use. For our final project I extended FoxRT and its Houdini add-on to work with scientific datasets from [ParaView](https://www.paraview.org/). The idea was to bring the data into Houdini, set up a scene there, and let FoxRT's physically based rendering make it look good.

The workflow was fairly straightforward: **ParaView → Houdini → FoxRT**.

<YouTube id="JM8tJEWO3Kw" title="CS6635 Scientific Visualization Final Project">
  A kite in a CFD simulation, rendered with FoxRT. This one was really fun to render.
</YouTube>

It was a nice change of pace from teapots, and a chance to use the same rendering infrastructure for something completely different. What started as an experiment in volumetric light transport had turned into a renderer I could keep extending and reusing.

## What's next

I'd like to keep working on FoxRT. The big items:

- **GPU support**, on hardware that accelerates ray traversal. It makes no sense to keep it CPU-bound.
- **Material formats** such as [OpenPBR](https://academysoftwarefoundation.github.io/OpenPBR/) and [MaterialX](https://materialx.org/).
- Maybe a **[Blender](https://www.blender.org/) add-on**, now that I know Blender better.

My other CS 6620 assignments are on the [course website](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/courses/cs6620/fall2025/).
